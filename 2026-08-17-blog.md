# Mountains at Both Ends of the World 

## New Zealand's Identity Crisis, Viewed from the Carpathians

---

Standing in the Făgăraș Mountains of Romania, looking out over forests that have been continuously inhabited for millennia, it is difficult not to think about the mountains at the other end of the world. The Southern Alps of New Zealand are younger, sharper, more glaciated — geological adolescents compared to the ancient, rounded, densely forested Carpathians. But the difference that strikes hardest is not geological. It is human. The Carpathians are a landscape people have been living inside for thousands of years. Shepherds' huts sit on trails walked since the medieval period. Villages occupy the same valleys they have occupied for centuries. The bears, the wolves, the forests, and the people exist in a relationship shaped by deep time. New Zealand's mountains, by contrast, are wilderness — magnificent, overwhelming, and not without human story but lightly marked compared to the Carpathians. 

New Zealand is a country still in the process of becoming itself. European settlement is barely seven generations deep — a blink by the standards of anywhere in Europe, let alone the ancient civilisations of Asia and the Middle East. The settlers who arrived from Britain in the nineteenth century came not to transplant their culture but to escape it. They wanted land, opportunity, distance from the class system. They were not peasants bound to a place for centuries, carrying folk traditions and seasonal rituals in their bones. They were mobile, aspirational, relatively educated people operating within a capitalist framework from the beginning. There was no equivalent of the Moldovan village grandmother making wine the way her great-grandmother did, because nobody had been anywhere long enough for that kind of continuity to develop.

What emerged instead was a practical, egalitarian, outdoors-oriented society that worked well but sat lightly on the land. The quarter-acre section, the rugby club, the local pub, the A&P show, the bach at the beach — these were genuine cultural forms, but they were sustained by specific economic conditions rather than deep tradition. When those conditions changed — when Rogernomics opened the economy in the 1980s, when the freezing works closed, when the small farms consolidated, when the local shops gave way to Pak'nSave and The Warehouse — the cultural practices they supported began to hollow out. The rugby club lost its base when the town lost its young men to Auckland and Australia. The country pub became either a tourist experience or a memory. The A&P show continues, but increasingly as heritage performance rather than an expression of a living agricultural community. You can still find pockets where this older New Zealand survives — the Christmas parade in New Brighton, a bowls club dinner in a small town, a pub in Hanmer Springs that hasn't been renovated — but pockets are by definition not the mainstream. A national identity that survives only in the margins is no longer a national identity.

Meanwhile, the country is being reshaped by forces that have no particular interest in what New Zealand used to be. Auckland, home to a third of the population, has been transformed by immigration into a Pacific Rim city — pleasant, diverse, expensive, and culturally interchangeable with others. The talent drain to Australia and beyond is structural, not cyclical, carrying away the ambitious and the capable in exchange for tourists and retirees. The media ecosystem has collapsed — no independent voice holding up a mirror and saying *this is who we are*. The commercial landscape is a branch office of Australia, which is itself a franchise of global capital. Walk down any New Zealand main street and count the locally owned businesses. 

Pākehā New Zealand — the European-descended majority — faces a peculiar version of this crisis. Unlike England, where a dominant ethnic identity is losing its grip and reacting with panic and far-right nationalism, New Zealand never articulated a European political identity in the first place. Being Pākehā was the default — the water the fish didn't notice. There was never blood-and-soil nationalism, never a deep ethnic mythology, never the cultural infrastructure for that kind of politics. So as the country diversifies, there is no resistance movement, no backlash, no equivalent of Brexit or the Front National. There is simply a quiet dissolution, with nobody quite noticing what is being lost because nobody had clearly named what it was.

At the same time, the Māori renaissance is asserting an indigenous identity — rooted, deep, connected to landscape and whakapapa, sustained by a language being revitalised and a cultural confidence higher than at any point since colonisation. Te reo is growing. Treaty settlements are reshaping governance. Māori culture is not performing itself for tourists; it is reasserting itself as a framework for understanding this place.

So where does New Zealand land? There are several possible futures, and the country may be heading toward all of them simultaneously.

The first is the Pacific nation. New Zealand stops trying to be a small European country at the bottom of the world and fully embraces being a Pacific Island nation that happens to have a large European-descended population. Māori culture becomes the cultural spine — not just acknowledged in ceremonial contexts but genuinely structuring how the country thinks about land, community, obligation, and identity. The relationship to place — to the mountains, the coast, the bush, the rivers — becomes the national story. Pacific and Asian populations integrate into this framework more naturally than they integrate into Pākehā culture, because it is based on relationship to place rather than ethnicity. This is roughly the trajectory of the last few decades, and it is the most hopeful option, though it requires Pākehā to accept a subordinate cultural role that many have not yet reckoned with.

The second is the Singapore model. New Zealand becomes a small, prosperous, multicultural technocracy where identity is essentially civic — good governance, clean environment, rule of law, quality of life. No deep cultural identity, but functional and pleasant. This is more or less what Christopher Luxon describes when he talks about New Zealand being the best small country in the world. It can work for a city-state. Whether it can work for a country with mountains and coastline and three thousand kilometres of ocean between itself and anywhere else is a different question. Singapore is extraordinary, but nobody would call it culturally rich. The risk is becoming a well-run hotel rather than a home.

The third is the accidental hybrid. Nobody plans it, but something genuinely new emerges from the mix — Māori frameworks, Pacific warmth, Asian pragmatism, Pākehā institutional culture, and the landscape itself as the binding element. This is the optimistic version, and it is what politicians invoke when they speak of New Zealand's unique multicultural identity. But it requires time and organic evolution rather than top-down nation-building. It could take another century to feel real rather than aspirational.

The fourth — and the one that current trends most strongly support — is the Australian drift. New Zealand retains its sovereignty, its flag, its seat at the United Nations, all the formal architecture of a distinct nation, while the lived experience of being New Zealand becomes indistinguishable from being a quieter, greener suburb of Australia. Not through any single decision, but through a slow convergence that nobody votes for and nobody particularly resists. The brain drain continues. The media ecosystem finishes collapsing. The commercial landscape completes its homogenisation. The All Blacks fade as a unifying force. Policy settings converge with Canberra's. The places that hold out — the West Coast, Southland, the tramping huts, the small towns too unfashionable for developers — become heritage pockets rather than the living culture. The country retains its stunning landscape and its clean air, but these become amenities rather than identity.

There may be a fifth option, one that draws from the best of the others and is anchored by the one thing New Zealand has that cannot be globalised, homogenised, or franchised: the land itself. The mountains, the coast, the bush, the rivers, the light. These are so distinctive, so overwhelming, so inescapable that they force a relationship on anyone who lives among them. You cannot live in New Zealand and not be shaped by the Southern Alps visible from your kitchen window, by the Tasman Sea breaking against the West Coast, by the beech forest that begins where the farmland ends. If New Zealand develops a deep identity — something that could not be mistaken for Australia or anywhere else — it will come from there — though even this is not guaranteed, as a quiet Australianification of the ecology itself is already underway.

The question is whether a relationship to landscape is enough to build a culture on, or whether you also need the human density and historical depth that places like Romania and Moldova possess. A Carpathian village has been accumulating meaning for a thousand years. A New Zealand valley has been settled by Europeans for a hundred and fifty. The Māori relationship to the land is ancient and profound, but it belongs to Māori, and grafting it onto a national identity without doing violence to its meaning is a task New Zealand has not yet figured out.

What is clear, standing in these old European mountains and thinking about those young Pacific ones, is that the window for New Zealand to become something distinctive is not infinite. The forces of convergence — economic, cultural, demographic — are accelerating, and they do not pause while a country works out who it is. Every year that passes without a clear sense of identity is a year in which the default answer — a pleasant, well-governed appendage of the Anglosphere — becomes a little more permanent.

The mountains will still be there. The question is whether anyone will remember they meant something.
